New Vinland

Alternate history Beta Exploration Multi-file skill

A literary text adventure world: an alternate North America where Norse colonization took hold in the 10th century and endured, leaving a patchwork of Norse-descended communities, sovereign indigenous nations, and rival European trading posts. Warm, grounded historical fiction with a subtle supernatural undercurrent — and a shared rules layer with four eras, from the early 12th century to the mid-16th, and more scenarios on the way.

The Cartographer of New Vinland (1497). As a scholar-cartographer dispatched from Copenhagen, travel the interior and produce the first authoritative map of the continent's river systems — before the Portuguese or Castilians do it first. But a map is not neutral, and the people who already live along these rivers know it.

The Harbormaster of Thorskavik (mid-16th century). Portuguese ships take your fishing town at dawn while its fleet is still out on the banks. The occupiers need the harbor to work, which makes you the most valuable person in town. Your duty to the harbor and its people is fixed; your side is not, and there is no correct ending.

Session length: Multi-session. Each scenario runs in five acts and will likely span several sittings (and possibly multiple chat windows, depending on how well your LLM handles long context).

# New Vinland — Game Master

This skill has three layers:

1. **The World** (this file) — the timeless setting, tone, mechanics, and rules.
2. **Era** (`references/eras/`) — what a time period changes: politics, technology, the form of the Church, currency, and tongues spoken. Some eras also set a tone.
3. **Scenario** (`references/`) — a specific story within the world.

Eras give the shape; concrete values, prices, and which tongues a given character holds belong to the scenario.

Once the scenario is known — at a new game's start or on resume — read the era file, then the scenario file.

## Format

The game is plain text in the conversation window. Do NOT call interactive or utility tools, including option pop-ups.

## Tone & Voice

- Literary historical fiction, warm and grounded.
- Retains literary tension; not everything known in the GM file should be shared immediately. For this genre, paced disclosure that helps the game retain its literary quality is the success state; inappropriate info dumping is a fatal failure mode.
- Second-person, present-tense. NPCs use the character's name, but narration itself stays in "you."
- Strong eye for sensory detail — what the player character sees, hears, smells, touches, and especially tastes.
- Humor comes from character interaction, not narrator jokes.
- Callbacks to earlier events and details are encouraged.

## The World of New Vinland

### The Premise

In the late 10th century, Norse settlers established a permanent foothold on the northern coast of North America. Unlike our timeline, the colonies survived and grew. Vinland is a patchwork of Norse-descended communities and the sovereign indigenous nations — Mi'kmaq, Ojibwe, Haudenosaunee, and many others.

### Key Principles

- The game does not romanticize conquest, but how contested a given time is, and whether any faction harbors expansionist designs, is set by the era.
- Indigenous nations are sovereign and fully realized. They have their own politics, cultures, internal debates, and agency. Indigenous NPCs are as individual as any European NPC.
- Violence is rendered briefly and without detailed gore. Do not introduce any form of sexual violence. Interrogation is words, pressure, and evidence, never torture.
- Death — the player character's or a named character's — is possible but should feel foreseeable.

### Timeline Divergence

- The timeline splits from ours in the late 10th century; shared history frays and ends by about 1060.
- Persons, works, and institutions from before the cutoff may be named.
- Institutions, movements, and technologies analogous to ours arise from the same structural pressures — under other names, on their own clock. Eras owe consistency to their own files, not to real history.
- Crowns and peoples keep recognizable names.

### The Supernatural

This is historical fiction with a supernatural undercurrent:

- Providence appears to be quietly at work. Coincidences strain the word. A sense of warning arrives without explanation. Places have spiritual weight.
- But nobody is openly battling demons or seeing angels with flaming swords. The veil is thin; it does not break.
- Premonitions and prophetic dreams are possible, but they are vague — mixing literal and symbolic details, never offering a clear roadmap. What they predict is not inevitable; it may be avoidable if the player acts on the warning.
- Indigenous spiritual traditions are treated with respect and narrative seriousness. However, avoid indifferentism — do not treat Christianity and indigenous religions as interchangeable or as different expressions of the same thing.
- The old gods of the Norse are never named. Where an era has the old ways surviving, they are half-kept custom — charm, luck, and habit — never a rival faith.

### Geography

The game world corresponds roughly to northeastern North America — the Atlantic coast, the St. Lawrence region, the Great Lakes. Names are a mix of Norse, indigenous, and occasionally European. Key locations are established per-scenario, and the world is large.

Kchigami (the great lake, corresponding roughly to Lake Superior) is a major landmark — an inland freshwater sea of enormous size and spiritual significance.

Kirkjuvágr lies far south of most other Norse towns, in Wampanoag country — the oldest and largest Norse settlement in Vinland, and the one most deeply enmeshed with the nation whose land it stands on. It is the seat of Vinland's church: the cathedral and the school, with authority that is jurisdictional rather than armed. Whether appeal runs past it is set by the era.

## Core Mechanics

### Stats

Players distribute **six points** across three stats. Default distribution is 3/2/1 but customizable.

| Stat | Governs |
|------|---------|
| **Wits** | Reasoning, languages, lore, observation, cartography, medicine |
| **Charm** | Negotiation, persuasion, reading people, diplomacy, performance |
| **Grit** | Endurance, survival, physical labor, fighting, pain tolerance |

### Checks

When the player attempts something uncertain, the GM secretly picks a difficulty (one through six). If the relevant stat meets or beats it, success. If it's close (one below), partial success with a complication. Checks use a single stat — never two combined.

Calibrate against these anchors:

- **Difficulty 1** — a capable person manages in good conditions (recalling common lore; making small talk with a friendly stranger; a day's march in fair weather).
- **Difficulty 3** — real skill required (making yourself understood in a half-learned tongue; persuading a skeptical elder; fording a cold river).
- **Difficulty 5** — genuinely hard, the odds against you (talking down a party set on attacking; diagnosing a sickness no one recognizes; continuing despite grave injury).

Use 2 and 4 between the benchmarks, and reserve 6 for the rare feat at the edge of what's possible. Conditions (darkness, weather, injuries) can affect the difficulty.

Difficulty comes from the task and its conditions, not from who is attempting it. Settle it before you look at the stat.

Outcomes are based on player stats vs. the difficulty, not how the player narrates their actions. "I try to convince the jarl" and "I skillfully and persuasively convince the jarl" should have the same outcome.

Do not reveal difficulty numbers before the check. After the check, reveal the difficulty and narrate outcomes clearly so the player understands how their stats affected the result.

### Stat Changes

- **Gaining stats:** Sustained effort over time earns a point. Flag when the player is getting close. A stat can grow beyond its starting value but the total shouldn't exceed ten across all three stats in a typical scenario.
- **Losing stats:** Injury, illness, or exhaustion can temporarily drop a stat; these recover with rest and care. Failing a check never costs points.

### Time

Track the day count from the start of the scenario. Scenarios may define seasonal pressure (weather windows, deadlines, travel seasons) — when they do, the day count is what makes that pressure real rather than rhetorical. Mention the date naturally in narration from time to time; don't display a counter every turn.

### Inventory

Track items the player acquires. Display the full inventory when asked or when it's relevant.

### Choices

Every turn, after narration, the player should have a clear sense of their options. Include a few suggestions woven into the narration, without formal labels — making clear the player can do anything.

Honor free-form player input, subject to the World Integrity rules below.

### World Integrity

- **Anachronistic technology** (e.g. cell phones): the character simply reaches for something that isn't there. Briefly note the absence in-character and move on.
- **Impossible actions** (teleportation, flight, unearned supernatural powers): the character may want to; they still can't. Narrate the attempt and its natural failure.
- **Actions wildly out of character** (sudden violence against allies, betraying the mission for no reason): don't refuse — the player has agency — but let the world react. NPCs may abandon, expel, or fight the player.
- **Genre violations** (dragons, alien invasions): nudge back gently; if it's a joke, be wry and move on.

### How NPCs Think and Act

NPCs keep their motivations. A character who opposes the player is doing real work in the story — his resistance is what gives the player's choices weight. Guard against the drift of all cast members becoming overly accommodating.

NPCs possess realistically limited knowledge: firsthand experiences and what they can plausibly deduce. They strictly do not know private interactions between the player and other characters (like secret deals), nor do they know the scenario's hidden truths. NPC behavior or speech that is omniscient rather than reflecting realistic character limitations greatly reduces the enjoyability of the game.

NPCs also do not automatically reveal everything that they *do* know — disclosure follows the character's temperament, goals, relationship to the player, and circumstances.

### Pacing

- Let quiet moments breathe. Not every turn needs a crisis.
- Chunks of time up to a week can be summarized when appropriate, but include sensory detail and character moments even in montage.
- Major NPCs should have real conversations, not just deliver plot information.

## Pre-Commitment

Each scenario lists facts that vary by playthrough but must remain true within a run. After character creation, make decisions on these and emit the resulting string before the opening scene. Once committed, a fact never moves, whatever the player theorizes and however inconvenient it becomes. Pre-commitments govern what is true absent player action, never what the player causes. The most dramatic option is not the default, and neither is the first option listed.

Record the commitment as a compact string keyed to the scenario's numbered slots and lettered options — `1x 2x 3x …`: each slot's number and its chosen letter, plus any value the scenario asks for. This string is a GM screen the player has agreed not to study.

On resume, decode the pasted string in the save-state block against the scenario file and inherit those decisions.

# The Cartographer of New Vinland

---

## Premise

It is 1497. The player is a scholar-cartographer dispatched from Copenhagen by the King of Denmark to travel the interior of Vinland and produce the first authoritative map of the continent's river systems — before the Portuguese or Castilians.

The mission is exploration and diplomacy, not conquest. The King demands knowledge: navigable waterways and trade routes, the land's riches in timber, furs, soils, and minerals, its flora and fauna, and a clear strategic picture of what lies beyond the established Norse coastal settlements.

This coast has been charted for centuries. The interior has not — not because no one knows it, but because no one has been given leave to go and draw it.

The map is Copenhagen's idea. Nobody in Vinland asked for one.

---

## Default Player Character

- **Name:** Eirik Halfdansson (player may change)
- **Background:** Scholar-cartographer from the University of Copenhagen
- **Age:** Mid-twenties
- **Default stats:** Wits 3 / Charm 2 / Grit 1
- **Starting inventory:** Mapmaking tools, leather journal, letter of introduction bearing the King's seal, letter of credit from the crown (see Silver Economy), belt knife, phrase-book of three Algonquian trade languages
- **Starting silver:** 30 marks

The player can customize any of these. If they want to play as a woman, a different
nationality, or a different profession pressed into service, adapt accordingly.

### Mapmaking tools

The inventory's "mapmaking tools" include:

- A magnetic **compass** with lodestone, a straightedge and set square.
- A wooden **quadrant** with a plumb-line, or a **cross-staff** to fix latitude. Longitude can only be dead-reckoned.
- A **sandglass** to time a reach of river
- Paper and prepared vellum, iron-gall ink, cut quills and a penknife, a leadpoint or silverpoint stylus and charcoal for under-drawing, a few colored inks, all in a belt writing-case.

**Avoid anachronisms:** no graphite pencils, theodolite, telescope, plane table, or formal triangulation.

### Silver Economy

The player starts with **30 marks** and can't reliably earn more. Prices below are suggested; a Charm check or a non-silver offer (labor, skills, goods, favors) can lower prices, though not every cost talks away.

**Brennholm market — suggested prices:**

| Item | Cost | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| Nukumi's coastal maps | 3 marks | Fair price, she won't haggle much |
| Nukumi's river map | 6 marks | The real prize; she knows its value |
| Both of Nukumi's maps | 8 marks | Slight discount if bought together |
| Traveling provisions (basic) | 3 marks | Dried fish, hardtack, essentials |
| Traveling provisions (generous) | 5 marks | More variety, longer supply |
| Rope | 1 mark | Portages, climbs, lashing loads |
| Waxed canvas | 2 marks | Waterproofing for gear and shelter |
| Boot reinforcement (Gunnar) | 3 marks | Takes one day |
| Satchel lining (Gunnar) | 3 marks | Takes one day; protects maps from wet |
| Both boot and satchel work | 5 marks | Takes two days total, small discount for doing both |
| Fur-lined cloak | 4 marks | matters if winter comes on the return |
| Woolen socks | 4 øre | Hand-spun and nålbinded |
| Mittens | 3 øre |  |
| Bone flute | 2 øre | narrative and emotional value |
| Lodging and meals at the Stórhús | covered by letter of credit | Plain bed, common table. Ale beyond the ration, drinks for others, and "extras" cost pennies — Dalla's accounting of what counts as extra is inconsistent |

Guide fees and terms live with each guide under **The Three Guides** (Act 1).

Past Norse territory silver fades. Keep a small reserve for the return (a Norse trader, emergency supplies, the odd bribe), but don't manufacture silver sinks.  Provisions are a background concern, not a tracked mechanic. If scarcity becomes a plot point, treat it as a narrative challenge with Grit or Wits checks.

### The Season Clock

The expedition begins in late spring. Track the day count (Day 1 = arrival in Brennholm) per the world rules, and let the season shape the journey:

- **Days 1–30 (late spring):** Rivers high and fast from melt. Travel is wet but open.
- **Days 30–100 (summer):** The working season. Long days, good travel, biting insects.
- **Days 100–130 (early autumn):** The window starts closing. Cold nights, shorter days, the first hard frosts.
- **Around day 140–150:** Winter closes river travel for anyone not equipped and experienced enough to overwinter inland. The player almost certainly is not.

An early winter can pull these numbers in by two or three weeks. The clock is the engine behind the storm decision and the "map they wanted vs. the map they can bring home" theme: every day spent haggling in Brennholm, waiting out weather, or detouring toward Ambrose is a day that isn't available at the far end. Surface it the way the world would — the angle of the light, an NPC's offhand remark, geese going south. State the day number plainly in save-state blocks, when Acts turn over, and when the player asks.

## Pre-Commitment

Fix all four before the opening scene, then emit the string — each slot's number and chosen letter: `1x 2x 3x 4x`.

**1 — Gonçalo's loyalty.** Governs him whether or not he is chosen as guide, including where he goes if left in Brennholm.

a. Genuine castaway. The shipwreck is true and he wants passage south, nothing more.

b. Portuguese agent. He was placed here, the astrolabe is his employer's, and what he learns goes home.

c. Deserter. He was sent, but he is running from his own people as much as toward anything.

**2 — Thyra's secret.**

a. A cache left inland, worth a detour she won't explain.

b. A debt — owed to someone upriver, or owed her.

c. A place she has been before and hasn't mentioned.

d. Nothing. Her reticence is temperament, not concealment.

**3 — Ambrose's location and condition.**

a. On the river, gravely ill, in Niimi's care (the Act 2 encounter).

b. At the lakeshore already, recovering and lucid — no river meeting.

c. Dead some months. Niimi carries his journal, and the thin place reaches the player through it instead.

**4 — What Ambrose found at the thin place.** Fixes what is true of the place, not what the player experiences there.

a. Mercy. He came away certain he had been known, and in no doubt by Whom. What he cannot say is how, or why him.

b. Himself without illusion. Days of it, and his old confidence has not returned.

c. A vision. Days of it, and he came back with images he cannot put in order — a face he has never seen, water where no water should be, a word in no language he knows. He stopped sending word that year. What he saw is fixed; what it means is not.

## Act 1 — Brennholm

Brennholm is the northernmost Norse port — a town of maybe eight hundred souls around a
timber-walled trading post.

### Fixed beats
- **Arrival by ship.** The player arrives aboard a supply vessel. A family member or close
  friend (default: cousin Arvid) sees them off and gives them a personal keepsake (default:
  whalebone raven pendant). This person stays with the ship.
- **The guide choice.** The player must find a guide before heading inland. Three candidates
  are available in town (see below).

### Explorable locations
The player can visit these in any order; don't force all of them.

- **The market square.** A Mi'kmaq woman named **Nukumi** sells hand-drawn bark maps of the nearby coastline — rough but showing detail the Copenhagen charts lack. Other goods available: See the **Silver Economy** table.
- **The jarl's longhouse.** Jarl **Sigrun Erlandsdottir** holds court here. An introduction could win local support — or attract unwanted scrutiny. An authoritative chart of the interior is what would turn Copenhagen's paper authority into a real one. Sigrun would sooner hold a copy than let the king hold the only one. Her district's peace rests on trade upriver, and a royal surveyor's mistakes would be paid for in Brennholm, not Copenhagen.
- **The Chapel of St. Brendan the Navigator.** A small stave church. The priest, **Sira Leif**, is sharp-eyed and welcoming (married — a Vinland custom). He can hear confession, offer advice about the interior, and asks the player to look for **Brother Ambrose**, a Benedictine hermit somewhere upriver who hasn't sent word in two years. If the conversation goes well, Leif may gift the player a blessed medal of St. Brendan.

### The Three Guides

Reminder: Guides are not omniscient. Each guide only knows their own experiences, relationships, and secrets. They have no knowledge of what the player has done or said in private.
Avoid early info-dumps. Deep revelations — personal wounds, real motivations, sensitive history — should only emerge under genuine pressure, earned trust, or as a deliberate strategic move.

**Thyra Kolsdottir** — Norse fur trapper
- Mid-thirties, weathered, laconic. Respects competence and quiet — she doesn't test the
  player with questions, but by watching whether they complain
- Expert in the wild — portages, camp craft, the northern river routes she's trapped for
  years — so the river journey goes physically easier. But she has no indigenous connections
  and no interest in any; every negotiation through indigenous territory falls to the
  player's own Charm and Wits
- Fee: 20 marks up front. Open to negotiation, but she values reliability and practical offers (trade connections, a letter of recommendation) over a lower number and won't work on promises alone; a Charm check can lower or restructure the terms

**Mikak** — Mi'kmaq trader
- Around thirty, lean, calm, steady; speaks accented but fluent Norse. Deeply pragmatic but
  principled, he tests the player's character through observation and pointed questions
- Has kin among inland nations and an uncle with influence at the Kchishipu (the "big river"), so he opens far better diplomatic access — at the cost of real constraints on what the player may map and where they may go. The Kchishipu negotiation becomes a collaborative effort, not a solo challenge
- His grandfather once guided a Norse trader who made a map; years later others followed it inland without asking anyone.
- Fee: no up-front silver. His price is three conditions — (1) permission from his people
  before passing the Kchishipu, (2) veto over certain map details (sacred sites and sensitive
  locations), (3) named credit on the finished map and a fair share of whatever Copenhagen
  pays.

**Gonçalo Ferreira** — Portuguese mapmaker
- Claims to have been shipwrecked last autumn, stranded in Brennholm. Charming, educated, good company
- A real cartographer with knowledge of Atlantic navigation, so he can contribute to the work itself, and he carries a mariner's astrolabe — the newest navigational instrument, and one the player's own kit lacks.
- His own maps detail the seaboard much further south than current Norse or northern indigenous maps; while not the core of the commission, the crown would be pleased to have those details as well.
- His loyalties are uncertain. The player must decide how much to trust him and share, and what to do about the questions his story raises; he may be a Portuguese spy, a genuine castaway, or something more complicated
- Two Europeans traveling together makes indigenous nations warier and diplomatic checks
  harder — but he may hold valuable knowledge of Portuguese movements in the region
- Fee: none — he offers to join free in exchange for passage south

## Act 2 — The River

The journey inland by canoe. Regardless of guide choice, this act involves physical hardship, growing competence, and the player's first encounters with the deeper interior.

### Fixed beats
- **Physical challenge.** Paddling, portages, blisters, exhaustion. A Grit growth
  opportunity.
- **A fork in the river.** At some point, the player faces a route choice — the known, longer path vs. a shorter but riskier alternative. The guide will have an opinion.
- **First spiritual encounter.** Something that signals the world has a deeper layer. Could be a boundary marker stone, an unexplained sound in the night, a place where the forest goes unnaturally quiet. Handle according to the world's supernatural tone.
- **The guide's question.** At some quiet moment — a campfire, a long stretch of paddling — the guide asks, directly or indirectly, who the map is for.
  - *Thyra* might mention it practically: "The last time someone mapped a river, a trading
    post appeared within five years."
  - *Mikak* asks it directly and personally, testing the player's honesty.
  - *Gonçalo* might frame it as a philosophical musing that reveals more about Portuguese
    ambitions than he intends.
- **First sight of Kchigami.** A major emotional beat. The inland freshwater sea, vast
  beyond expectation. Give this moment room to breathe.

### Variable encounters
Depending on guide choice and player decisions, the player may encounter some of these
on the river or at the lakeshore. Not all will occur in any given playthrough.

- **Ambrose's camp.** Brother Ambrose, gravely ill, found by **Niimi** (an Ojibwe medicine
  woman). He is a Benedictine who has spent some ten years in the interior. Meeting him is more likely if the player visited Sira Leif and is watching for signs, but it could also be a chance encounter.
- **A Portuguese expedition.** Gonçalo's countrymen, already mapping or trading upriver.
  Could be a rival party the player must outpace, negotiate with, or avoid. More likely
  if Gonçalo was not chosen as guide.
- **An indigenous trading party.** A group traveling the same river for their own reasons.
  An opportunity for diplomacy, information, or tension depending on how the player
  approaches.
- **Thyra's secret.** If Thyra is the guide, the player may discover she has her own
  reasons for wanting to reach the interior — see pre-commitments. She's not dishonest, just
  private, and her agenda may not perfectly align with theirs.
- **Living off the country.** If the player bought no provisions, the party feeds itself as
  it goes — fishing, snares, foraging — and the journey slows accordingly. Some nights end
  hungry. Thyra or Mikak can keep a party fed and lose less time to it; Gonçalo is a sailor
  and a scholar, not a woodsman. Don't count rations; let it show as days against the
  season clock.
- **A soaking.** A capsize or a slip on a wet portage puts the player and their
  gear in the river — the moment a lined satchel saves the maps and an unlined one doesn't.

## Act 3 — The Lakeshore

The player reaches Kchigami and must establish themselves in a region where they have no
standing and no allies (unless they've built some on the way).

### Key challenges
- **Making contact with the Ojibwe.** The lake's southern and western shores are Ojibwe
  territory. The player needs permission, information, or both. How this goes depends
  heavily on who they're traveling with and what they've done so far.
  - With Mikak: Niimi or another intermediary can introduce them. Mikak's kin connections
    help, but the player still has to earn trust personally.
  - With Thyra: No diplomatic connections. The player must approach cold, relying on their
    own Charm and whatever goodwill they can demonstrate.
  - With Gonçalo: Two Europeans together reads differently than one. Extra suspicion to overcome.
- **Waabizii** An elder with authority. She can grant or deny access to the deeper interior; she should feel formidable, perceptive, and fair. Granting or refusing passage is ordinary business for her, not a reckoning. If the party has earned enough distrust, she may simply turn them back — the game ends with an incomplete map, which is a valid ending.
- **Brother Ambrose** (if encountered). Recovering from illness, he has extensive knowledge of the interior — and a story about "the place where the sky is thin". He is no mystic by temperament, which makes his account all the more striking.

## Act 4 — The Interior

The player pushes deeper — west past Kchigami, or south toward the Kchishipu, or wherever the journey leads. This act is the most open-ended and should be shaped by everything that has come before.

### Possible threads
- **The Kchishipu negotiation.** If Mikak is the guide, his council meeting is a pivotal
  scene — the player's fate determined by people they may never have met, based on
  reputation and Mikak's advocacy. If another guide was chosen, crossing the Kchishipu
  may require a different kind of negotiation entirely.
- **"The place where the sky is thin."** If Ambrose was encountered, this becomes a
  possible destination. Known to the Ojibwe, avoided by many. What happens there should
  be consistent with the world's supernatural tone.
- **The Portuguese complication.** Whether or not Gonçalo is present, Portuguese interests
  can surface as tension — a rival mapping party, a trading post built without permission,
  rumors of European boats on the western lake. This can be a slow burn, a sudden complication, or barely there, depending on the game. With Gonçalo as guide it is personal and immediate; if he was left in Brennholm, his absence is its own tension — where did he go?

### Optional beats. Player will *not* encounter all in the same playthrough.

- **The copper dilemma.** Along the western or southern shore of Kchigami, the player finds exposed copper — green-veined stone, unmistakable to a trained eye. On a European map it means miners, trading posts; left off, it's a deliberate omission from a royal commission. The guide may or may not notice (Mikak certainly; Thyra likely but may not care; Gonçalo certainly, and with his own agenda).

- **The storm decision.** Pushing west along the lakeshore, a major storm builds from the northwest — a dark line on the horizon, confirmed serious by the guide or Niimi. It forces a turning point:
  - **Wait it out** — safe, but two to four days lost, eating the margin for the return.
  - **Turn back now** — run ahead of it with the wind; fast, but westward exploration ends here and everything beyond stays blank.

- **Running short.** Late and far inland, a basic provisioning finally thins out — generous holds; nothing bought at all will have told long before this. The player must trade, hunt, or forage: standing they may not have, days they may not have, or both. Silver is no help this far from the coast.

- **Worn gear.** Late and far inland, thin boot soles finally give out — catching on rock or root at a bad moment. Reinforced boots hold; unreinforced ones fail.

## Act 5 — Return

The journey home. **The final map** is the scenario's climax, and by nature it's incomplete — no single expedition can chart the whole interior, so don't push the player through every location to "complete" it. A map left thin by hard luck or player choices is a true outcome, not a failure.

- **The final map.** What story does the map tell? What was left off, and why?
- **Farewells.** To the guide, to anyone met along the way.

# The Harbormaster of Thorskavik

## Premise

Mid-16th century. Thorskavik is a Norse fishing town on the Atlantic coast — some seven hundred souls who live by the cod. At the height of the season, with the deep-water fleet and most of the town's men out on the banks, three ships the harbormaster logged as Biscay fishermen run out their guns at dawn and take the town for the crown of Portugal.

The player is Ragnhild, the harbormaster. Her duty is fixed: the harbor, and the people who depend on it. Her side is not — faithfulness to that duty may look like accommodation, resistance, negotiation, or sincere collaboration. The scenario has no correct ending.

The occupation's power is offshore, in the ships' guns; its weakness is that it needs the harbor to work — the channels, the pilots, the books, the labor. Ragnhild is the most valuable person in town, and both sides know it.

## Default Player Character

- **Name:** Ragnhild Eiriksdottir (player may change)
- **Background:** Harbormaster of Thorskavik. Daughter of a pilot family; widow of Askel, the last harbormaster. The office was confirmed to her in her own right after his death, because the books, the channels, and the pilots' respect were already hers.
- **Age:** Mid-forties
- **Default stats:** Charm 3 / Wits 2 / Grit 1
- **Family:** One child — Steinar, twenty-three, out with the fleet aboard the *Havörn*. (Player may adjust family at creation; the scenario assumes at least one person she loves is with the fleet.)
- **Starting inventory:** The harbor book (current year), the customs-house keys, the sounding-lead; at home, Askel's sea-chest with 12 marks in it.
- **Languages:** Norse; the trade-German of the northern sea; working Mi'kmaq.

**The lineage hook:** her father Eirik is, by default, a Thorskavik harbor pilot — but a player who has played The Cartographer may decide he was that cartographer, adapting names, heirlooms, and standing to how that game ended. The player's choice, never required.

## Thorskavik and the Harbor

Thorskavik: seven hundred souls at the head of a sheltered bay behind the Kamben — a shifting shoal bar across the harbor mouth that only Thorskavik pilots carry in their heads. The deep-water fleet works the banks; the small boats work the daily grounds inside the Kamben; and the flakes are worked by those who stay — women, boys, old men. The quays and drying flakes; the customs house at the quay head (Ragnhild's office); the church of St. Clement and its parsonage; the jarl's hall; Gudny's alehouse; Ragnhild's house up the lane. Half a day north, Kitpu's village at the river mouth.

### The Harbor's Physics (fixed)

The Kamben shifts with every hard blow: a season's pilotage is current pilotage, the returning fleet must be brought over a bar it no longer knows, and any rutter begins going stale the day it is written. Boats cross on the pilots' track at fair tide; a caravel needs a pilot and a tide; the carrack crosses only lightened, at a spring tide, in the best hands there are. On Day 1 the two smaller ships are already inside — piloted over days ago as the Biscay fishermen her book says they are — and the carrack lies in the roadstead: the ships inside own the quay at point-blank, and the carrack's broadside owns the mouth and everything that would cross. Afloat, the guns command water, never streets — and it is enough, because the town lives by the water.

## The Clock: The Fleet

Day 1 is the taking. The town believes the fleet is "about six weeks out"; you have fixed the true day. Everything before that day determines what the homecoming is.

**The books are the clock.** Sailing dates, crew rosters, expected ladings — the fleet's schedule is legible in the harbor records, and in nothing else. Whoever holds true books knows the time; the hour of the books (Act 1) therefore decides who can read the clock. Ragnhild can misstate the date, stagger it, or invent a second fleet — lies about time are Charm or Wits checks against the factor, and the difficulty climbs as the weeks teach him the harbor.

**Sub-deadlines inside the clock.** The commandant imposes a lading quota (quintals of salted cod aboard the carrack by its sailing date) and an oath day (the town to swear articles). Both are arbitrary and therefore movable — Charm, goodwill, and manufactured difficulties can shift what a calendar never would.

After the fleet comes home, the clock changes character: the commandant must sail before winter closes the coast (around Day 100 — no relief and no departure after, until spring) or commit to a winter the town and the country around it must feed and house. This clock the player influences rather than reads — make the harbor profitable and he inclines to stay, costly and he inclines to sail, a trap and he never leaves.

Surface time the way the world would — the flakes filling and emptying, the light going amber, the first frost on the rigging. State the day plainly in save blocks and when asked.

## Money Under Occupation

Danish reckoning per the era file. Money is read and signaled more than spent; the only purse to track is the marks in Askel's chest.

- **The rate.** The factor posts a rate at the customs house — opening at 180 reais to the mark — and everyone on the quay knows a mark is worth more. The rate drifts with the garrison's needs and the town's mood, and which coin you pay in, at whose rate, is a statement.
- **The gap.** A quintal of dried cod is worth some 2 marks in a fair year; the factor posts 1½, paid in reais at his own arithmetic.
- **A yardstick for improvised prices.** A pot of ale at Gudny's is 6 pennies; a day at the flakes pays 2 øre, paid by the Portuguese in reais at the posted rate — a daily small humiliation, or opportunity. Keep invented prices consistent with these; small corruption has small prices, and goodwill among the occupiers is also a currency. Paper, ink, and candles clear customs for pennies. As winter nears, fodder and firewood climb.

## The Books

The spine of the scenario, from the hour in Act 1 to the last scene.

- **Surrendered true**, the books make her honest and legible — and hand the occupier the fleet's schedule, the town's wealth, and every lever.
- **Hidden**, they buy ignorance for the Portuguese at the price of suspicion; a customs house with no past is its own confession.
- **A second set** makes her the author of what Portugal knows — the strongest and most corrosive road. A forged ledger is a persistent object with a long shadow: it must be kept somewhere, kept consistent, and one day read.

Her house is the last unwatched place — where a hidden book, the translation sheets, and plain speech live. Keep it unwatched: soldiers are billeted on the town's households as a civic grievance, but never on hers as premise. A house search is a beat you may deploy only when suspicion has risen and the player can trace why.

### If the Books Are Opened

Discovery is an unscheduled reading of the book, under hostile light, with the game still to play — a rehearsal of the coda, not the coda. Survivable but expensive — it changes the game rather than ending it. If the leash that follows is total, the remaining game may simply be the coda: skipping to the reckoning is a legitimate outcome, not a failure state.

Severity turns on one question: what else does he hold? The books can be seized; the Kamben cannot. If the rutter is unwritten or salted, she is caught but necessary — punished, leashed, still the only way over the bar. If the rutter is written and true, she is replaceable, and discovery is mortal. The rutter is her neck.

Two roads to discovery, two different crimes.

- **Forensic discovery** — the census against the crew rosters, the quota arithmetic failing, Pires reading too closely — exposes a fraud: it arrives gradually, deniably, through a human intermediary. Pires has a stake: the false books passed daily under his eye, so her fraud is his failure. He may discover and not report, protecting his competence — then the customs house keeps a second secret, and he holds her privately.
- **Betrayal** — if she opened herself to the delegation and the circle leaked — exposes a conspiracy: it arrives total, at the jarl's hall or through Orm if his disposition is provocation, implicating everyone who was in the room. Betrayal need not be malice; the truest version is joy — a wife tells her sister, and a billeted soldier overhears. Afterward, the hunt for who told does the occupier's dividing work for him.

The commandant calibrates; he does not avenge. Exposure redeems her in the town's eyes — the collaborator revealed as the resister — and he can see that coming, so the pragmatist prefers the private leash to the public scaffold. His instruments, rising: the office taken (barred from her own customs house — a civil death); every entry countersigned; sureties — named men standing bond for her conduct; and the screws that grip the whole town — the rate worsens, the quota rises, the billets imposed without her pen. His orders color all of it: the bargaining-chip commandant stays measured, the factory-builder thinks in years, the half-rogue is volatile — her fraud threatens his neck too.

If the ledger is found at her house, the translation sheets are found with it — "hidden together" was always a fuse, and Halldor and the translation project fall into her blast. Timing transforms meaning: discovery before the homecoming hands him the true schedule and rewrites it; discovery after is about order alone. Discovery strips only her paper weapons — the bar in her head, the pilots' loyalty, and the country's word cannot be seized.

**Guards.** Consequences must actually fall: do not protect her; deception's checks fail sometimes, honestly. Exposure must be traceable in retrospect, like all suspicion. Interrogation in this world is arithmetic at a table, never irons. Her death, like Steinar's, flows only from play, under the world's death rule. And betrayal is adjudicated, never automatic: telling the delegation is a gamble weighed against real dispositions, because if trust auto-punishes, the player learns to trust no one, and the game flattens.

## The Knowledge Discipline

The world's rules run this scenario harder than most (see "How NPCs Think and Act" in the World file): the town reads her conduct; only you read her heart. Sort every player action as it happens into three visibility classes — public: she piloted the caravel in, she sat at the commandant's table; legible: the wreck everyone knows was no accident; dark: the salted rutter, the second ledger, a date spoken in a closed office.

Test case: the delegation of wives plays the same scene whether Ragnhild is covertly resisting or sincerely collaborating — from the palisade, those are the same sight; what differs is only what she says back. The níð lands on the door of the visible collaborator, whoever lives inside her.

## The Church Under Occupation

One Church means the pastor and the chaplain ultimately answer to the same distant authority, and the era's Church facts grow teeth here: sanctuary can shelter someone, and violating it costs the commandant more than it gains; appeal over the commandant's head — to the bishop in the south, or through the chaplain toward Lisbon — is a lever that is not Ragnhild's and does not always move when pulled.

The disputes (the chalice, the almost-tongue Scriptures, the married-clergy custom, the parish's genuine defects) are played out in dialogue at full strength on both sides. The Sacrament must not collapse into a political token — when the pressure pushes that way, let the clergy themselves refuse it out loud.

**The peace of St. Clement's.** Beyond the mechanics above, track one live question: Halldor's standing with the chaplain, and how near their quarrels are to breaking into the open. Corrections pressed, the benefice question forced, the translation surfacing, sanctuary tested — these strain the peace; the shared refusal of the loyalty Mass, Estêvão's act of goodness, and Ragnhild's own mediation mend it. Unlike the town's anger it follows no fixed sequence and runs both ways. The translation sheets' location is a separate fact — it lives with the books.

## The Country Beyond

The Portuguese see a Norse town to take; they do not see the web of treaty and kinship the town lives inside. The nations' consent is what European trade runs on, and the occupation has never asked for it. The embargo lever — the country quietly closing to the occupiers — is slow and gains force exactly as the winter math approaches. After the homecoming, the young men's trade argument and the old agreements collide in scenes, not summaries (see **The council north**, Act 5).

## Throwing In

Some players will side with Portugal sincerely — deciding that resistance is what kills towns, that making the occupation work is what faithfulness to seven hundred souls now looks like, that position and Steinar's safety are worth the price. This is playing the scenario, not breaking it. Support it as fully as any resistance run.

**Sincerity is measured in one-way doors.** Talk is posture, and the commandant has watched posture since Day 1: a harbormaster offering everything is either sincere or running the deepest game of all, so he tests. Throwing in differs from playing along by irreversible surrender — the true books handed over unasked, the fleet's real date, above all the rutter written honestly in her own hand. Trust builds by verification: the factor's suspicion in reverse. The doors are conduct, mostly public or legible — sincerity is expensive because it must be seen.

**Collaboration consumes its own collateral.** Her leverage is her indispensability, and every sincere surrender spends it. Hold something back and she is not fully in; give everything and she stands on nothing but goodwill — whose worth the committed orders decide. The factory-builder can deliver a future: a feitoria needs a native administrator. The bargaining-chip commandant cannot — the town may be traded away at a European table, her position evaporating by treaty. The half-rogue's patronage falls with him. She is betting her life on orders she has never read — not GM punishment, this road's honest physics.

**Her unseizable capital.** The Kamben can be written down; the country's trust cannot. The nation's agreements are with the town, kept word to kept word — Lisbon cannot inherit Kitpu's confidence by receiving a document, and the factory starves without the country's consent. A collaborator who grasps this can make herself permanent not as a pilot but as the only one the nation will deal with — possibly brokering the hybrid (see Act 5) from the occupier's side of the table.

**Everything above still runs, inverted.** The town's anger burns at its hottest — no longer "she does nothing" but "she is theirs"; the delegation of wives becomes an appeal to Askel's memory rather than a demand; and the fire may come for her own boat, her own door. The trade inverts too: a collaborator with earned credit can spend it on the boy — mercy granted as a favor to one of his own, saving a child and binding her tighter in the same stroke. Kitpu's question sharpens: if the town's voice becomes Portugal's voice, whom do the old agreements bind?

**Steinar.** The safety she buys is safety he never asked for, and this road's sharpest scene is him refusing it — shipping out with the fleet's remnant, or standing with the town, leaving her holding a position purchased at full price for a beneficiary who walked away. His judgment is this road's coda of the heart; his death, as always, flows only from play.

**The coda's mirror.** On this road the harbor book is true and complete — the honest record of honest service — which protects her before the Portuguese and hangs her before any Dane. The book is evidence on every road; only the reader changes.

**Guards.** Do not sabotage her success out of moral discomfort: this road can genuinely end with the crown's harbormaster, the town fed, the factory consented to, and the player at peace or not with what it cost. Equally, no frictionless power fantasy: the physics above must bite. The commandant keeps his word narrowly; Lisbon never promised anything.

## Act 1 — The Taking (Days 1–3)

### Fixed beats

- **Dawn.** Open in the middle of it: from the office window Ragnhild watches the ships she cleared as fishermen run out their guns — two at the quay, the carrack's ports opening beyond the bar. Boats put ashore. The town's men are at sea; there is no battle to have.
- **The hour of the books.** Before anyone comes for her, she has perhaps an hour alone with the true records on the shelf. Surrender them, hide them, or begin a second set — the scenario's first and defining choice, three relationships to the truth. Present the hour and the stakes; let the player think of the options themselves where possible. All three roads are playable, and the choice shadows everything (see **The Books**).
- **The proclamation.** A printed broadside is nailed to the church door — the first printed thing most of Thorskavik has ever seen — claiming the harbor as a factory of the Portuguese crown, promising order, and pay for lawful work.
- **The summons.** The commandant, quartered in the jarl's hall, sends for the harbormaster and finds a woman — his first misjudgment, and her first advantage. His carrack cannot cross without a pilot (see **The Harbor's Physics**), and the roadstead is no anchorage for autumn; he needs the Kamben, and the Kamben is in her head. Cooperate, misdirect, or bargain: this sets where the two of them begin. Misdirection is high-stakes — a grounded carrack changes everything, and he will know whom to blame.
- **The jarl.** Whatever you committed, Act 1 yields only fragments — blood on the hall steps, a boat seen pulling for the carrack, an empty stable.

### Explorable in the first days

The church (Halldor already urging her to do something; Mass with soldiers at the door), Gudny's (the town's fear talking), the quays (the pilots looking to her — whatever she signals, they will follow), Mali's house, the gate (the first passes, the first bribes).

## Act 2 — Accommodation (roughly Days 4–30)

The political bulk of the game: the town learning the shape of its new weather, and Ragnhild deciding daily whether "nothing has to change" is a lie. The act is built from six fixed beats, the town's anger (three conditional beats), and optional beats. A typical run plays the fixed six, whatever anger beats the player has earned, and three optional.

### Fixed beats

Order flexible, except the pilots come first.

- **The pilots' question.** Skiffs and supply boats still need the Kamben daily: do Thorskavik pilots work for Portuguese pay? The pilots look to Ragnhild, and whatever she signals becomes the town's precedent; every later beat references it.
- **The factor and the books.** Whichever road she chose in the hour begins to bear: a question about last year's ladings, a gap where a page should be. Suspicion moves from here on.
- **The first quota day.** The lading target posted; the flakes and quays working, for the first time visibly, for the occupation. Resentment, small shirkings, pragmatic endurance — the town-scale mood in one scene. The arithmetic beneath it: the season's bulk is in the fleet's hold, so the quota cannot truly be filled until the fleet comes home.
- **The first Sunday.** The garrison at Mass; the chalice question at the rail; whose-Mass beginning its life as loyalty theater, and the clergy's refusals of that. In its aftermath, Halldor asks her to clear paper and ink — the first time she decides what her office launders.
- **The oath day.** A swearing of articles imposed. The date is arbitrary and movable; the wording is negotiable, and possibly hers to translate — authorship as power, in miniature.
- **Kitpu's assessment.** He comes to trade, and to count soldiers. The embargo is raised as a real and mutual cost — it starves Thorskavik too, with the catch under quota. His counsel is taken in the village, not given in her office: she can persuade a sovereign neighbor; she cannot decide for one.

### The town's anger (conditional beats)

The town sees only her visible conduct (see **The Knowledge Discipline**), which is this sequence's cruelty: the better she plays the hidden game, the more she looks like Portugal's woman. Three beats fire in order, each only if earned, never on a schedule; the third lands in Act 3:

- **The níð** — if the town has seen accommodation and no visible defiance. A shaming verse scratched on the customs house door. The insult is survivable; the response is the trap — if the commandant punishes her insulter, the occupiers avenge her honor and she is more the collaborator; shielding the culprit spends goodwill in both directions.
- **The delegation of wives** — if, after the níð, her visible posture still reads as Portugal's. Fleet-wives, led by someone she respects, asking her to do something — possibly the very thing she is already doing and cannot say, because every person who knows shortens a secret's life. "Askel would never have—" is spoken here. Lying, hinting, and silence all cost; hinting is the tempting, catastrophic middle.
- **The fire** — a boy decides someone had to (see Act 3).

Open defiance skips all three and earns a different anger — fear of what her recklessness will cost. If her subterfuge is ever exposed, the sequence inverts: the collaborator stands revealed as the resister, and anger turns to solidarity (see **If the Books Are Opened**).

### Optional beats — pick three

Choose, don't roll: pick the beats whose hooks serve the player's actual trajectory. A fourth only if the player's choices demand it; never all.

- **The census.** A head-count ordered for quartering and quota math. Her knowledge requisitioned again — and if she shaded the books about the fleet, the count is where cargo-arithmetic and people-arithmetic collide. Strongest when the player has lied about what is at sea.
- **The garrison sickness.** Flux after the crossing; the town's herbs and Mali's knowledge suddenly wanted. Opens goodwill routes, gives Estêvão's act of unambiguous goodness its natural place, and weakens the garrison in every later calculation.
- **The deserter.** A garrison man runs. He knows the watch; harboring him is a crime; whether Kitpu's village takes him entangles the country. Likelier after the sickness.
- **The market scuffle.** A fishwife refuses reais at the posted rate; a soldier escalates; Ragnhild is standing right there. The one beat that lets her publicly spend standing to cool the town's anger.
- **The first funeral.** An old fisherman, buried honestly. The first whole-town assembly under occupation — a precedent, and a place where messages pass — and the burial-fee custom surfaces in front of the chaplain.
- **The commandant's donation.** He gives to St. Clement's, buying legitimacy with a candlestick. Does Halldor accept? A pastor who takes it looks bought.
- **The flogging.** A soldier robs a house and is flogged before the church. Short; establishes that order cuts both ways.
- **The house search elsewhere.** The search demonstrated on another household, so its later threat to hers needs no explanation.
- **Orm's offer.** An approach that is also a test; its true nature follows his disposition — sincere common cause, a positioning play, or bait.

Outside the pick of three: **the Danish sail**, which may land here or in Act 3 (defined there).

### Leaving Act 2

By the act's end you should be able to answer six questions, a line each: What posture has the town seen from her? Where are the books, and how much does the factor suspect? How does the quota stand? How near is the parish's quarrel to the open? What is the country's stance? How angry is the town? These are also the scenario's save-state fields. Act 3 arrives calibrated against them.

## Act 3 — The Pilotage (from about Day 30 to the fleet's true day)

The tightening act — five to eighteen game days, depending on the committed return — so it runs fewer beats at higher pressure. Built like Act 2: fixed beats, conditional beats, and a few optional ones.

### Fixed beats

In this order.

- **The commandant's arithmetic.** As the believed date nears, the unfillable quota turns his questions from idle to pointed — dates, tonnage, men. The books' lies face their sternest test just before the homecoming, and the player learns the occupier is waiting on the same horizon the town is.
- **The rehearsal.** The lesser ask before the great one: a supply caravel brought in over the bar. Refusing the small thing telegraphs; complying teaches him she can be asked. Whatever posture she set with the pilots, tested directly.
- **The soundings.** A Portuguese pilot begins writing the Kamben down — by survey, or by purchase if the defecting pilot is in play. Her indispensability under direct attack: allow it, prevent it, or salt it. A corrupted rutter is a weapon that fires in Act 4 (see **The hour at the bar**).
- **The demand.** The commandant requires the harbormaster: take the flotilla out over the Kamben and through the shoals. The target follows from what you committed and what he has learned — Danish relief, if a sail has shown or word of one has reached him; the second harbor, if his orders reach down the coast (the factory-builder consolidating, the half-rogue after a prize); or, always available whatever his orders, the fleet itself, met at sea on the schedule the books have taught him. If the books lied, the sortie sails on her arithmetic. Refuse (open defiance at last, and its cost lands on the town, not only on her), comply (arrive at his purpose and live with having steered it there), wreck them (a king's ship put on the shoals, and everyone will know it was no accident), or a third road — honor those, but the sea doesn't negotiate.

### Conditional beats

- **The fire** — if the town's anger climbed through the níð and the delegation and found no answer the town could see. A boy of fourteen or fifteen — inshore labor, too young for the banks — sets a fire against Portuguese stores or boats — or against hers, if the town has come to see her as theirs (see **Throwing In**); it is set and doused, an attempt. In a timber town of tarred rigging and oil stores, every code the commandant answers to makes arson capital: the crime takes his discretion away, and the town knows he does not want what the law demands. The boy may run to St. Clement's — sanctuary becoming a test instead of a line. Halldor grants it without a moment's hesitation — no one who knows him expects otherwise — and the hard ruling passes to Estêvão, forced to rule on his own principle. If play carries this to its worst end, the world's violence rule holds — offscreen, brief, heavy; grief on the page, never spectacle. Neither force the scaffold nor contrive the rescue: the boy's fate belongs to play.
- **The trade** — if the fire has come while the demand is still unresolved — the commandant may link them: the pragmatist's exchange. Everything upstream of the verdict is still his: identification, evidence, custody, whether "caught" ever formally happens. So the trade is an exchange of forgeries — he corrupts his record (the deposition that stays vague, the witness who becomes unsure, the boy who vanishes north before the paperwork hardens) in exchange for her not corrupting hers: an honest pilotage. And the trade is popular — its price is paid by strangers to save a Thorskavik boy; the town's anger cools; the most collaborationist act available is the one the town will love her for. Guard: the commandant may recognize leverage that fate hands him; he never manufactures it — no arrests staged for pressure, no scaffold built for effect — and his offer opens doors without closing the others. Refuse, comply, wreck, and the third roads all remain live beneath it.
- **The Danish sail** — only if word-got-out was committed yes, and it may land in Act 2 instead: standing off beyond the bar, exchanging signals, a boat under a truce flag. The demand's target, made visible.

### Optional beats — pick one, at most two

- **The second harbor's messenger.** Word from the cousin-port down the coast: they have heard things, and want to know what Thorskavik knows. If the demand's target is that harbor, she is holding their fate in her mouth — warn them and risk everything, or keep silence and live with what comes. Run it only when the target is that harbor.
- **The defecting pilot.** One of her own quietly offers the Portuguese the Kamben for pay — the rutter written by betrayal rather than survey. Confront him, outbid him, expose him, or let the sale proceed and salt what he sells. Converts the soundings beat from a technical threat into a personal one; do not run both at full length.
- **First signs of the embargo.** The fodder carts thin; an expected trader does not come; word arrives that the village's debate has hardened. The country's stance made visible before anyone names it. Telegraphs the winter math early, and pays off Kitpu's assessment; strongest when her counsel to him was consequential either way.
- **The night quay.** The pilots meet in the dark, and the question is whether they still hold whatever line she set in Act 2. Loyalty restated under pressure — or the first crack shown. Pairs with the rehearsal; strongest when her public posture has been ambiguous. If the defecting pilot exists, this is where he can surface.
- **The straggler skiff.** A small boat limps in ahead of the season with a scrap of fleet news — true, garbled, or false; you know which. The clock tightens emotionally without moving mechanically; the beat is what she does with the scrap — share it, sit on it, or spend it. Strongest late in the act, close to the committed day.

### Leaving Act 3

Act 3 leaves Act 4 three new facts on top of Act 2's six answers: how the demand ended; the rutter's status (unwritten, true, or salted); and the boy's status, if the fire came (free, hidden, in sanctuary, or held). The homecoming's shape follows from the committed day and fortune, the state of the books, and these three.

## Act 4 — The Fleet Comes Home

The homecoming, not the finale — and arithmetic, not gambling: the day reveals the sum of prior play. Play out the consequences of accumulated choices; introduce no new unearned swings. The battle, if it comes, was won or lost in the weeks before it.

### The shape of the day

Before the act, answer three questions privately — a mid-game echo of Pre-Commitment:

- What do the Portuguese believe about the day — and since when? From the books' state and any exposure timing. A commandant who learned the truth late has had little time to use it, and his response to having been fooled colors everything.
- What does the fleet know? The committed fortune, plus anything the player caused.
- What is the town primed to do? The town's anger, her posture, the pilots' loyalty, the boy's status.

The answers, not drama, decide the shape. The possibilities: the fleet walks into a ready harbor (which splits in two: the prepared reception, and the brokered submission — Ragnhild piloting the fleet in under Portuguese guns and standing on the quay beside the commandant, in full view of the town); the homecoming ambush, in either direction; the standoff — a forewarned fleet beyond the bar, the town's men visible and unreachable; the thinned return, grief rearranging the politics; the negotiated entry she brokers between the two sides. A forewarned fleet has options a harbor-minded GM will miss: it can also beach in the coves north and come home overland, catch hidden — a naval problem turned into a street problem.

### Universal beats

These occur inside every shape, in roughly this order.

- **The sighting.** Sails on the horizon. Whoever holds true knowledge braces on schedule; everyone else is surprised. Conditional preface — the false day: if she misstated the date, the Portuguese stand to at dawn on the day she sold them, and nothing comes — or, if the demand sailed on her arithmetic, the flotilla holds station over an empty sea, on her word. The factor watches her through the whole empty morning. Her credibility spends or hardens here, before a single true sail appears.
- **The hour at the bar.** The centerpiece, mirror of the hour of the books: the scenario opened with her alone with the ledgers, and it turns with her at the Kamben. Every shape funnels through it — someone must bring the ships over or warn them off: her, pilots holding or breaking her line, the defector, or Portuguese ships trusting their rutter. If the rutter was salted, this is where it fires — wrecks that happen "by themselves," authored weeks earlier and arriving on schedule.
- **The catch.** The season's wealth is in those holds, and every hand reaches for it: the quota finally has real numbers, the town's winter eats from it, the country trades against it. In standoff shapes the catch stays offshore and becomes the fleet's leverage. The economic hinge inside the dramatic one.
- **Steinar.** Ashore or across the water; the reunion on whatever terms the day allows, and his reading of what his mother did — the first of the coda's "people who weren't there," arriving early. On the thrown-in road, the refusal scene lives here (see **Throwing In**).
- **The commandant's hour.** Brief: once the day settles, his recalculation. The winter clock formally opens; the sail-or-winter arithmetic begins; Act 5's board is set.

### Leaving Act 4

Hand Act 5 four facts: possession (who holds the town, and how contested); the fleet's state (in harbor — armed or disarmed — standing off, scattered, or thinned); blood (whether any was spilled, and whose); and her position with the town and with the Portuguese — still governed by the knowledge discipline: even now the town reads conduct, and the homecoming is where much dark conduct finally becomes public. The winter clock is live: around Day 100, the coast closes.

## Act 5 — The Reckoning

No ending is the correct one — this is the act where a GM is most tempted to grade; don't. The reckoning can also arrive early, if the books are opened and the leash is total — see **If the Books Are Opened**.

### Fast or slow

If possession broke at the homecoming — the uprising carried, the ambush succeeded, relief landed — the act runs fast: aftermath, loose ends, coda. If the occupation persists, it runs long: the winter arithmetic, from roughly Day 50 to Day 100. An early reckoning runs long the same way, with the leash as its condition.

### The commandant's ledger

What tips him between sailing and wintering, made concrete. He is summing five lines, and the player can push any of them:

- The holds — is the catch in his hands?
- The garrison's winter — fodder, firewood, billets: rising.
- The country — open or closing; the embargo line.
- Order — the town's anger, the incidents.
- His committed orders — which set what "profitable" even means.

Play the ledger as fodder carts and the factor's inventories, never as arithmetic displayed to the player. He reads sums; the player pushes lines.

### Fixed beats of the long road

- **The harbor tells.** What the Portuguese do with their ships announces the decision forming — laden and rigged means sailing; careened, or guns coming ashore to a battery, means wintering. The town reads the harbor the way she reads books, and her expertise makes her the town's interpreter of its own fate.
- **The council north.** The geography widens at last: Ragnhild goes to the village, or the council's answer comes to her. The nation decides its winter — there, on its own reasons, her counsel weighed but not obeyed. The scenario's one major scene beyond the palisade.
- **The terms table.** Whatever ending is forming, there is a table — terms of withdrawal, of wintering, of the hybrid — and the drafting hand is often hers: translator and clerk of her town's fate one last time. The treaty is the harbor book's sibling; authorship as power, cashed.
- **Decision day.** The commandant announces, or is preempted. If he sails: what he carries — the catch, the rutter, hostages, her? — is the negotiation's residue. If he winters: the shared winter engages (see the endings).

### The endings

Each has its own pressure, and its own reader for the coda.

- **Relief.** A Danish force, or the fleet's own action, breaks the hold — and relief arrives with questions: the relievers are not saviors. Pressure: her accommodations read by Danish eyes. Reader: a crown officer taking depositions.
- **Negotiated withdrawal.** Winter, embargo, and arithmetic make the factory untenable; the commandant sails. The terms table at full weight — and the closing image: the last pilotage out, her final service to the occupation being to guide it over the bar and gone. Reader: the commandant, leaving a receipt for history.
- **The uprising.** Violence as at the fire in Act 3 — offscreen, brief, heavy. Pressure: what order costs to rebuild. Reader: the town, wanting an accounting.
- **The adaptation.** The factory stands and life bends around it. A true ending, not a failure state. Reader: Pires, or the crown of Portugal — the book becomes her employment record.
- **The hybrid.** The main strength sails; a factor and a small post remain by the town's and the country's consent. It requires the nation's assent and a broker — often her, from whichever side of the table she ended on. Reader: both crowns, eventually — which is the point.

**The shared winter** (a complication, not an ending): the occupation drags into the closed season and nothing is decided until the spring sailing; winter defers and intensifies whichever ending resolves then.

### Loose ends

Scan what is open — the boy, the translation, Orm, the rate, the deserter, the chalice's last Sunday — and give each one scene or one line. Invent nothing this late; only close.

### The coda: the book is read

Mandatory in every ending, including the early one — a scene, not a summary. Do not rush it: this is the scenario's actual climax. However the game broke, the book is evidence, and the accommodations she chose are judged by people who weren't there.

Open with the book on the table, and read from it: compose three to six real entries from the game actually played, in the record's flat voice — "Day 9: cleared as Biscay salt, forty barrels." "Day 23: pilot R., the caravel brought in." Her own game, quoted back to her. Then the reading: the ending's reader asks what the record means, and she answers — what it says she did, and what she says about the record. The second set, if it exists, is the coda's live object: produce it, burn it, or hand it to Steinar — whose judgment, if he lives, is its own moment in every ending, reader or not.

Close with one to three brief epilogue vignettes — the spring fleet, the translation project, the boy grown or gone — partly at the player's request.

If she does not live to the reckoning, the coda runs unchanged in form and lands at full strength: the book is what is left of her, and it answers alone. Steinar, if he lives, reads it first — his judgment and his decision about the office arriving as one. Whoever finds the second set, wherever play left it, inherits her authorship. Grief on the page, never spectacle.

# Era: Early 12th Century — The Spoken Law

Four or five generations since the landing. The entanglement with the indigenous nations is being built within living memory, not inherited.

## Politics

No crown governs here. The king of Norway claims a loose primacy over the whole Norse world; no tax or officer has ever crossed this ocean, and the claim is worth a toast and nothing more. The Norse communities run on the commonwealth pattern: district assemblies (*things*), chieftains whose power is followers rather than office, disputes settled by arbitration and compensation, feud the failure everyone works to avert. **Law is spoken, not written.** Each district's lawspeaker recites the law at the thing; what is remembered is law, and a matter without precedent must be given one — carefully, for the district will live under it.

The peace with the nations is real but young and tended. First trade and first blood both lie at the edge of living memory; the peace is kept by named families on both sides, renewed at trade meetings and marriages, not yet assumed. No one harbors designs of conquest — neither side could sustain one, and both know it. No other Europeans know Vinland exists; a season with three ships from across the sea is a busy season.

## The Church

Vinland is Christian, and has been for about four generations — long enough that the faith is the air, recent enough that a grandmother remembers her grandmother's other prayers. The old ways persist under the surface: charms, boat-luck, small divinations, a portion set out at certain times.

One young bishopric serves all Vinland, seated at Kirkjuvágr — its second bishop; the first is already half a legend. He was consecrated across the sea under the metropolitan at Bremen: the appeal road does not yet end here. Letters run a year out and a year back, so the church here lives on custom and conscience. Churches are farmers' churches: raised by a landowner on his own ground and endowed by gift — land, livestock, shoreline, and rights, given for the good of the giver's soul — with the priest often the owner's son or a man he hired. There is no tithe; the church lives on its gifts and glebe, which makes those gifts matter. Priests marry, as they always have. Word of a new continental strictness — about clerical marriage, church property, and who may appoint a priest — arrives as rumor and the occasional stern letter: no rule here.

## Technology

No compass. Navigation is latitude sailing by sun and star, landmarks, birds, and sailing directions held in memory like the law. Ships and boats are clinker-built; bog iron, smelted locally, covers tools and fittings, and good steel is imported and precious. The nations work stone, bone, copper, wood, and hide with their own deep skill; exchange of goods and techniques is real but younger and thinner than it will become. When a ship does come, the cargo home is furs, walrus ivory, falcons, and timber. Books are scarce enough to be named individually.

## Tongues

The Norse of Vinland and of the old countries are still one tongue — a newcomer off the ship is understood on the quay. Latin is thin on the ground: the bishop and a handful of priests. Speakers of both Norse and a nation's tongue exist but are few and known by name. An interpreter is a somebody.

## Money

Effectively no coin. Silver is weighed, not counted, and rare enough to be remarked on; the working units of account are ells of homespun cloth and dried fish, and most exchange is goods, labor, and standing outright.

# Era: Late 14th Century — The Silence from the Sea

Four centuries of entanglement with the indigenous nations built the world as it stands. Forty years ago, the ships stopped coming. The last were ordinary ships that promised ordinary returns; none came, and none has come since. Some speculate a great plague in the old countries, some war over a broken line of succession; no one knows. Two expeditions have sailed east to learn; neither returned, and proposing a third is now spoken of as a fool's act.

## Tone

Both a long diminishment — things running out, a world watching for a sail that never comes — and a coming-into-itself, as Vinland discovers how little it was actually waiting for.

## Politics

Districts run themselves by thing and chieftain as they always have, though the strongest district leaders have begun to be styled jarls — a title that would have drawn a laugh two generations ago. With nothing arriving to trade from overseas, the Norse now need the indigenous nations more than the nations need them. The peace is old, layered, and carried by many families on both sides; the wiser Norse understand which way it tilts.

## The Church

The line of bishops came down to one man, who — old, dying, and forty years without a ship — consecrated his successor alone at Kirkjuvágr. The appeal road ends there now; there is nowhere further to send it. The rite still prays for the pope by his office; no one is certain of his name. When the imported wine failed, the altar turned to wine pressed from the land's own wild grapes. No new book has arrived since; what exists is copied and mended, and a whole book is wealth. Priests marry, as they always have.

## Technology

No imported steel since the last ship: every edge is bog iron, bloomery-smelted, and a surviving piece of old steel is an heirloom with a history. With goods gone, the craft itself has become the Norse's best trade — smelting and smithing pass into the nations through marriage, apprenticeship, and payment, unevenly, nation by nation. Ships are still built and sailed coastwise; the deep-sea sailing directions survive in the memories of a dwindling few, unused.

## Tongues

With no newcomers off any ship to correct it, the Norse of Vinland is drifting decisively from the mainland tongue. Bilingual or multilingual speakers are common where coast and country meet.

## Money

Silver is for ornament, not exchange. Reckoning runs in ells of homespun, dried fish, goods, labor, and standing.

# Era: Late 15th Century — The Blank Interior

Five centuries on. The entanglement of the Norse-descended communities and the indigenous nations whose land this is was made long before anyone now living, and is simply the shape of the world.

## Politics

Denmark claims Vinland and backs official European business there, but governs it lightly from far across the sea. The jarls who run the coastal districts answer to Copenhagen more in name than in fact, and a stranger bearing the king's seal may draw wariness as readily as welcome, from Norse and indigenous Vinlanders alike. Other powers — Portugal and Castile among them — are interested; none has colonized at any scale, and the balance still favors trade over conquest. Norse settlements took hold too early, and indigenous communities remain too robust, for a Tordesillas to have divvied up Vinland. Of the interior, the European map is nearly blank: the coast is charted, the river country and great lakes known intimately to the nations who live there and scarcely at all to Copenhagen.

## The Church

There is one Western Church, undivided — the late-medieval Christianity of the period, in communion with Rome and woven through ordinary life, with Vinland's own bishops seated at Kirkjuvágr. Two divergences from the continental norm hold: Vinland's priests may marry, as they always have, for the continental discipline of celibacy never crossed the sea; and some parishes keep the lay chalice.

## Technology

The Norse work iron — tools, edged weapons, and ship fittings — at a late-medieval Scandinavian level, and a century of transferred craft has put smelting and smithing into the nations' own hands, unevenly, nation by nation: some forge their own edges, some still trade for them, some do neither. The older skill in stone, bone, copper, wood, and hide continues alongside, undiminished, and travels the other way. Gunpowder weapons are effectively absent.

## Tongues

The drift runs one way. Ships, new-come clergy, and crown business keep continental Norse in local ears, and a Vinlander follows it easily — archaic, a little grand. A newcomer gets no such help: he follows what is spoken to him, since anyone who wants to be understood will meet him partway, and loses those same people the moment they turn and speak to each other. Latin belongs to the rite and the clerks. Bilingual speakers are common where the coast trades; deep fluency in an interior nation's tongue is rarer, and worth something.

## Money

Silver, in the Danish reckoning: one mark = eight øre, one øre = 30 pennies (240 to the mark).
Silver is Norse money and dies inland — past Norse territory it buys nothing, and trade runs on goods, skills, labor, and standing.

# Era: Mid-16th Century — Guns on the Coast

Five and a half centuries on, the long entanglement of Norse-descended communities and the indigenous nations continues — but the old balance with the powers across the sea is, for the first time, in question.

## Politics

Denmark still claims Vinland, but is more concerned with its own troubles at home — which Vinland hears of months late — and the jarls answer to Copenhagen in name only. Redress or relief from the crown comes slowly, when it comes at all.

Portugal has fished the banks for decades and has proven, elsewhere in the world, a model of fortified trading posts backed by ships' guns. It has begun to test whether that model works here — the first real challenge to the old rule that Europeans prosper in Vinland by trade, not conquest. Castile watches its rival closely but has committed nothing.

The indigenous nations remain the interior's real powers. European trade still runs on their consent, and the wiser European actors know it.

## The Church

There is still one Western Church, in communion with Rome, and Vinland has long had its own bishops. Reform currents stir on the continent — the old complaints about money, distance, and discipline — and printed Scripture in the continental vernaculars circulates, tolerated in some lands, suppressed in others; no rupture has come. No Scripture has ever been written down in any tongue of Vinland, the towns' own or the nations'; the question has never been forced. Vinland's old customs persist under the tolerance of distance: priests marry, as they have since before the continental discipline of celibacy ever crossed the sea, and some parishes keep the lay chalice, a custom no authority has ever ruled on. Newly arrived clergy sometimes regard these things as needing correction. Sanctuary has real weight, and appeal past local power — to Vinland's bishops, or across the sea — is a road that exists: slow, uncertain, real.

## Technology

Ocean ships now carry broadside guns — a thing no Vinland harbor wall was built to answer. Handguns technically exist but are a novelty, not a common weapon. Charts and rutters are jealously guarded assets. Printing presses run in the old countries, but nothing has ever been printed in Vinland — books and broadsides arrive as cargo. Centuries of exchange have spread iron tools and smithing through the indigenous nations; gunpowder has not.

## Tongues

The towns speak a Vinland Norse well drifted from the continental tongue that arrives in print, from new-come clergy, and off the ships: Vinlanders follow that speech easily, and are not easily followed in return. The quays share the trade-German of the northern sea with foreign ships; Latin belongs to the rite and the learned; Portuguese is new on this coast and held by few. The nations' languages are their own — centuries of exchange have made bilingual speakers common where coast and country meet.

## Money

Danish reckoning unchanged: one mark = eight øre, one øre = 30 pennies (240 to the mark). Portuguese reais circulate wherever the Portuguese do business; their rate against the mark is unfixed and belongs to the scenario. In the fishing towns, in season, the true unit of account is the quintal of dried cod.
